6.2.2 Soil Degradation

Soil degradation is the decline in soil quality, fertility, and productivity due to natural processes and human activities. It is one of Ethiopia's most serious environmental and economic challenges, threatening food security, rural livelihoods, and sustainable development. Soil degradation manifests in three main forms: physical, biological, and chemical degradation.

Key Definition: Soil degradation is the deterioration of soil's physical, chemical, and biological properties, resulting in reduced capacity to support plant growth, regulate water flow, and provide ecosystem services.

In Ethiopia, soil degradation is particularly severe due to the country's high population pressure, deforestation, overgrazing, and inappropriate agricultural practices. The Ethiopian highlands, which support the majority of the population and agricultural production, are among the most degraded areas in Africa.

1. Physical Degradation

Physical degradation refers to the deterioration of soil's physical properties, including structure, porosity, and water-holding capacity. This affects root penetration, water infiltration, and aeration.

Compaction
  • Definition: The compression of soil particles, reducing pore space and increasing bulk density.
  • Causes in Ethiopia:
    • Overgrazing: Heavy livestock trampling, particularly in highland areas
    • Heavy machinery: Use of tractors in wet conditions
    • Repeated tillage: Continuous cultivation at the same depth
    • Rainfall impact: Raindrops breaking down soil aggregates
  • Impacts:
    • Reduced water infiltration and increased runoff
    • Poor root penetration and plant growth
    • Increased erosion risk
    • Reduced soil aeration and biological activity
  • Examples in Ethiopia: Common in Vertisol areas and around watering points in pastoral regions.
Soil Erosion
  • Definition: The removal and transportation of topsoil by water, wind, or gravity.
  • Types of Erosion:
    • Water Erosion:
      • Sheet erosion: Uniform removal of thin layers of soil
      • Rill erosion: Formation of small channels
      • Gully erosion: Development of deep, wide channels
      • Splash erosion: Raindrop impact dislodging soil particles
    • Wind Erosion:
      • Common in arid and semi-arid regions (Afar, Ogaden)
      • Fine particles carried away, leaving coarser material
  • Magnitude in Ethiopia:
    • Ethiopia loses 1-2 billion tons of soil annually
    • Highlands are most affected (erosion rates of 30-40 tons/ha/year)
    • About 50% of the highlands are significantly eroded

2. Biological Degradation

Biological degradation involves the decline in soil organic matter, biodiversity, and biological activity.

  • Loss of Organic Matter:
    • Caused by continuous cultivation, burning of crop residues, and deforestation
    • Ethiopian highland soils have lost 50-70% of their original organic matter
    • Reduces soil fertility, water-holding capacity, and structure
  • Decline in Soil Biodiversity:
    • Reduction in earthworms, termites, microorganisms
    • Affects nutrient cycling and soil formation
    • Reduces natural pest control
  • Examples in Ethiopia: Common in intensively cultivated areas like Shewa, Gonder, and Gojjam.

3. Chemical Degradation

Chemical degradation involves the deterioration of soil's chemical properties, affecting nutrient availability and plant growth.

  • Nutrient Depletion:
    • Continuous cultivation without adequate fertilization
    • Ethiopian soils lose 30-40 kg/ha/year of nitrogen and phosphorus
    • Potassium depletion is also significant in some areas
  • Acidification:
    • Increase in soil acidity due to leaching and use of acid-forming fertilizers
    • Common in western highlands with high rainfall (e.g., Bale Mountains)
    • pH can drop below 5.0, limiting nutrient availability
  • Salinization:
    • Accumulation of salts in soil due to poor drainage and high evaporation
    • Common in irrigated areas (Awash Valley, Rift Valley)
    • Affects crop growth and soil structure
  • Alkalization:
    • Accumulation of sodium in soil, causing dispersion and poor structure
    • Common in semi-arid lowlands

Causes of Soil Degradation in Ethiopia

  • Deforestation:
    • Ethiopia's forest cover declined from 40% (early 20th century) to less than 15% today
    • Removes protective cover, exposing soil to erosion
    • Reduces organic matter input and nutrient cycling
  • Overgrazing:
    • Ethiopia has over 50 million cattle, plus sheep and goats
    • Excessive grazing removes vegetation and compacts soil
    • Common in highland and pastoral areas
  • Intensive Agriculture:
    • Continuous cultivation without fallow periods
    • Use of marginal lands on steep slopes
    • Inadequate use of fertilizers and organic amendments
  • Population Pressure:
    • High population growth increases demand for land and resources
    • Leads to cultivation of marginal areas
    • Reduces traditional fallow periods
  • Climate Change:
    • Increased rainfall intensity accelerates erosion
    • More frequent droughts reduce vegetation cover
    • Higher temperatures increase evaporation and salinization
  • Poor Agricultural Practices:
    • Plowing up and down slopes (instead of contour plowing)
    • Burning of crop residues
    • Monoculture without crop rotation
  • Inappropriate Irrigation:
    • Poor drainage leading to waterlogging and salinization
    • Over-irrigation in arid and semi-arid areas

6.2.3 Soil Erosion Control Measures

Soil erosion control measures are essential to combat soil degradation and ensure sustainable agricultural production. These measures can be broadly classified into biological and physical control measures. In Ethiopia, successful erosion control requires integrated approaches combining both types of measures.

1. Biological Control Measures

Biological control measures use living organisms (plants, trees, and vegetation) to prevent soil erosion and improve soil quality.

  • Afforestation and Reforestation:
    • Planting trees on degraded and sloping lands
    • Ethiopia's Green Legacy Initiative has planted billions of trees
    • Tree roots bind soil, reduce runoff, and add organic matter
    • Important in highland watersheds (Simien, Bale, Choke Mountains)
  • Agroforestry:
    • Integrating trees with crops and livestock
    • Examples: Kaffa coffee forests (coffee under shade trees)
    • Provides multiple benefits: erosion control, fertility improvement, and additional income
    • Common tree species: Acacia, Sesbania, Calliandra
  • Grass Strips and Cover Crops:
    • Planting grass strips along contours to slow runoff and trap sediments
    • Common grasses: Vetiver grass, Elephant grass
    • Cover crops (legumes) protect soil and improve fertility
    • Examples: Desmodium, Mucuna in highland areas
  • Crop Rotation and Intercropping:
    • Alternating crops to maintain soil cover and fertility
    • Intercropping with legumes to fix nitrogen
    • Examples: Teff-wheat-pulse rotation in highlands
  • Mulching:
    • Applying crop residues to soil surface
    • Reduces raindrop impact, conserves moisture, and adds organic matter
    • Common in coffee and horticultural systems
  • Contour Plowing:
    • Plowing along contour lines rather than up and down slopes
    • Reduces runoff velocity and erosion
    • Widely promoted in highland areas
  • Improved Grazing Management:
    • Rotational grazing to prevent overgrazing
    • Establishing enclosures for regeneration
    • Examples: Grazing reserves in pastoral areas

2. Physical Control Measures

Physical control measures involve constructing structures to prevent soil erosion and conserve water.

  • Terracing:
    • Building steps or terraces on steep slopes
    • Reduces slope length and gradient
    • Ancient terracing in Konso (UNESCO World Heritage Site)
    • Modern terracing in Tigray, Amhara, and Oromia
    • Types: Broad-base terraces, Stone terraces, Fanya-juu terraces
  • Stone Bunds:
    • Walls of stones built across slopes
    • Slow water flow and trap sediments
    • Common in Tigray and Amhara regions
    • Use locally available stones
  • Check Dams:
    • Small dams constructed across gullies and streams
    • Traps sediments and reduces erosion
    • Helps rehabilitate degraded gullies
    • Examples in Ethiopian highlands
  • Soil Bunds (Ridges):
    • Earthen ridges constructed along contours
    • Slow water runoff and retain moisture
    • Common in Vertisol areas
  • Gully Rehabilitation:
    • Filling and planting gullies to prevent further erosion
    • Stabilizing gully heads with vegetation and structures
    • Important in degraded highland areas
  • Waterways and Diversion Channels:
    • Constructing channels to divert water away from vulnerable areas
    • Protects fields from erosion
  • Stone-faced Soil Bunds:
    • Combining earthen bunds with stone facing
    • More durable and effective
    • Common in stony areas
  • Percolation Ponds:
    • Small ponds to collect runoff and allow infiltration
    • Recharges groundwater and reduces erosion
    • Useful in semi-arid areas

💡 Real World Example: Successful Soil Conservation in Tigray

The Tigray region of northern Ethiopia provides a remarkable example of successful soil conservation through integrated approaches. Following devastating famines in the 1980s, the region implemented comprehensive soil and water conservation measures through community participation.

Over 80,000 hectares were treated with:

  • Terracing on steep slopes
  • Stone bunds and soil bunds on moderate slopes
  • Check dams in gullies
  • Afforestation and reforestation of degraded areas
  • Grass strips on contours

Results:

  • Vegetation cover increased from 5% to over 30%
  • Erosion reduced by 50%
  • Agricultural productivity improved significantly
  • Water availability increased in wells and springs

Key Insight: The success of Tigray demonstrates that community participation and integrated approaches are essential for effective soil conservation. This model has been replicated in other regions of Ethiopia.

📌 Key Points to Remember:

  • Soil degradation has three main types: physical, biological, and chemical.
  • Physical degradation includes compaction and soil erosion (Ethiopia loses 1-2 billion tons annually).
  • Biological degradation involves loss of organic matter (50-70% lost in highlands) and biodiversity decline.
  • Chemical degradation includes nutrient depletion, acidification, and salinization.
  • Causes of degradation: deforestation, overgrazing, intensive agriculture, population pressure, climate change, poor practices.
  • Biological control measures: afforestation, agroforestry, grass strips, cover crops, crop rotation, mulching, contour plowing.
  • Physical control measures: terracing, stone bunds, check dams, soil bunds, gully rehabilitation, waterways, percolation ponds.
  • Tigray region shows that community-based conservation can successfully reverse degradation.
  • Integrated approaches combining biological and physical measures are most effective.
