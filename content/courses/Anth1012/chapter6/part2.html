6.3 Women’s role in conflict resolution and peacemaking

Ethiopian women participate in the process of dispute settlement in exceptional cases. For example, in some cultures, women participate in dispute settlement processes when cases are related to marriage and women's issues. Despite these weaknesses, women are not completely excluded for indigenous systems of governance, conflict resolution, and peacemaking activities. In some societies, women use their own institutions to exercise power, protect their rights, and actively participate in peacemaking activities.

InstitutionEthnic GroupDescription
The Yakka & Siqqo Sidama
  • Yakka: Women's association or unity group.
  • Siqqo: A stick that symbolizes peace and women's honor.
  • Mobilizing the Yakka and holding the Siqqo, Sidama women stand for their customary rights.
  • If a woman is beaten by her husband, the women's group imposes a fine (e.g., an ox). The Yakka leader mobilizes the group to the man's house, holding the Siqqo, shouting and singing.
  • If found guilty, the man is forced to slaughter a sheep and give part of it to the Yakka.
  • Sidama women also use their Siqqo to make peace between quarrelling parties.
  • Oromo women have a similar peace stick called Sinqee, which serves the purpose of protecting women's rights and making peace.
Don Kachel Agnuak (Nuer, Mejenger, Opo, Komo)
  • Literally means "let us all live in peace".
  • A peace-making movement initiated by Jaye, a group of wise and elderly Agnuak women.
  • Based on information gathered through women's networking (including gossip), the Jaye call disputing parties for a meeting.
  • After hearing arguments, the Jaye give their verdict. The party that caused the conflict requests forgiveness and pays compensation.
  • A sheep or goat is slaughtered; the meat is cooked and shared by participants.
  • The Jaye announce: "the problem resolved, let us all live in peace together!"
The Debarte Tigray (Raya-Azebo)
  • An institution of reconciliation established by elderly and highly respected women in Raya-Azebo, Tigray.
  • Plays an important role in avoiding harms associated with the culture of revenge.
  • When a man kills another man, the wife of the killer requests the Debarte intervention.
  • The Debarte instruct the killer's wife to gather her female relatives, wearing their netela upside down and covering their hair with black cloths to show grief and regret.
  • The women walk to the home of the murdered man, crying and uttering: "Abyetye ezgio! Abyetye ezgio! (Oh God help us!)"
  • Upon arrival, the Debarte kneel down and cover their heads with the dust of the compound, begging for forgiveness.
  • Initially, the victim's relatives may refuse, but they eventually change their mind and open the door for reconciliation.
  • After persuading the victim's relatives to give up revenge, the Debarte give the way for elders who start the peace-making process.

6.4 Legal pluralism: interrelations between customary, religious and state legal systems

Legal pluralism is an important concept in disciplines that study legal issues. It refers to the existence of two or more legal or justice systems in a given society or country. Legal pluralism indicates the co-existence of multiple legal systems working side-by-side in the same society. Pluralism as a normative concept also refers to a system that recognizes other norms emanating outside state institutions along with a state legal standards.

Legal Pluralism in Ethiopia:

Legal pluralism is evident in the Ethiopian context as well. The existence of multiple ethnic groups in Ethiopia has not only made the country home to diverse cultures but also a place of diverse legal systems. Multiple legal institutions, including customary laws and courts, state laws and courts, and religious laws and courts (e.g., the Sharia Law), work side-by-side in most parts of the country. The 1995 Constitution of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia (FDRE) also grants specific rights to ethnic groups, which ultimately involves the recognition of indigenous legal systems.

Constitutional Provisions:

The FDRE Constitution provides some spaces for religious and customary laws and courts to address personal and family cases. The following two Articles show this reality:

  • Article 34(4): In accordance with provisions to be specified by law, a law giving recognition to marriage concluded under systems of religious or customary laws may be enacted.
  • Article 78(5): Religious and customary courts that had state recognition and functioning prior to the adoption of the Constitution shall be organized on the basis of recognition accorded to them by the Constitution.

Legal Hybridism:

Legal pluralism is a pervasive phenomenon in Ethiopia. This is because a single legal system does not have a capability to address all legal cases and maintaining peace and order. Contemporary studies indicate that the relation between the various legal systems is characterized by cooperation and competition. The interactions of legal systems also display contradictory perspectives and cross into territories claimed by the other. And hence, the legal sphere seems characterized by legal hybridism indicating one cannot stand without the cooperation of the other legal systems.

The following justice institutions work side-by-side in most parts of the country, especially in remote and rural areas:

  • State/formal justice institutions: Including state law, district court, and the police.
  • Customary institutions: Including dere woga/customary law, dubusha/customary court, and dere cima/council of elders.
  • Social courts: Attached to each kebele administration.

In regions such as Afar, Somali, and Harari, religious courts such as the Sharia court are also very important.
