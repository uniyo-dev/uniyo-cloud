1.1.3. Compound (or Complex) Propositions

In mathematics, just as we combine numbers with arithmetic operations (like \(+\) and \(\times\)) to form complex expressions, we combine propositions with logical connectives to form compound propositions. A compound proposition is formed by joining two or more propositions using logical connectives. The truth value of a compound proposition depends entirely on the truth values of its individual components and the rules of the connectives.

📌 Definition 1.2:

The proposition formed by joining two or more propositions by connective(s) is called a compound statement.

Why Order Matters:

We must be careful to insert brackets in proper places, just as we do in arithmetic. For example, the expression \(p \Rightarrow q \land r\) will be meaningless unless we know which connective should apply first. It could mean \((p \Rightarrow q) \land r\) or \(p \Rightarrow (q \land r)\), which are very different propositions.

Convention for Order of Operations:

When dealing with compound propositions, we adopt the following convention on the use of parentheses. Whenever \(\vee\) or \(\wedge\) occur with \(\Rightarrow\) or \(\Leftrightarrow\), we assume that \(\vee\) or \(\wedge\) is applied first, and then \(\Rightarrow\) or \(\Leftrightarrow\) is applied. For example:

  • \(p \wedge q \Rightarrow r\) means \((p \wedge q) \Rightarrow r\)
  • \(p \vee q \Leftrightarrow r\) means \((p \vee q) \Leftrightarrow r\)
  • \(\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p\) means \((\neg q) \Rightarrow (\neg p)\)
  • \(\neg q \Rightarrow p \Leftrightarrow r\) means \(((\neg q) \Rightarrow p) \Leftrightarrow r\)

⚠️ Critical Exam Tip:

Although the convention exists, it is always advisable to use brackets to explicitly indicate the desired order. This prevents any ambiguity and ensures you are evaluating the correct proposition—especially important in exam settings!

Truth Tables for Compound Propositions

The possible truth values of a proposition are often listed in a table, called a truth table. If \(p\) and \(q\) are propositions, then there are four possible combinations of truth values for \(p\) and \(q\): TT, TF, FT, and FF. If a third proposition \(r\) is involved, then there are eight possible combinations of truth values for \(p\), \(q\), and \(r\).

General Rule: A truth table involving \(n\) propositions \(p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\) contains \(2^n\) possible combinations of truth values. Such a table will have \(n\) columns (for the variables) and \(2^n\) rows. We use truth tables to determine the truth value of a compound proposition based on the truth values of its constituent component propositions.

Example 1.6 (a):

Suppose \(p\) and \(r\) are true, and \(q\) and \(s\) are false. What is the truth value of \((p \land q) \Rightarrow (r \lor s)\)?

  • Step 1: Since \(p\) is true and \(q\) is false, \(p \land q\) is false.
  • Step 2: Since \(r\) is true and \(s\) is false, \(r \lor s\) is true.
  • Step 3: Apply the rule of implication: \(F \Rightarrow T\) is true.

Therefore, \((p \land q) \Rightarrow (r \lor s)\) is true.

Example 1.6 (b):

Suppose a compound proposition is \((p \lor q) \Rightarrow (r \Leftrightarrow \neg s)\), and the truth values of \(p, q, r, s\) are \(T, F, F, T\), respectively.

  • \(p \lor q\) is \(T\) (since \(p\) is true).
  • \(\neg s\) is \(F\) (since \(s\) is true, its negation is false).
  • \(r \Leftrightarrow \neg s\) is \(T\) (since \(r\) is \(F\) and \(\neg s\) is \(F\), they are the same).
  • Thus, \((p \lor q) \Rightarrow (r \Leftrightarrow \neg s)\) is \(T \Rightarrow T\), which is true.

Logical Equivalence

In logic, two propositions may look different but actually mean the same thing. This is called logical equivalence.

📌 Definition 1.3:

Two compound propositions \(P\) and \(Q\) are said to be equivalent if they have the same truth value for all possible combinations of truth values for the component propositions occurring in both \(P\) and \(Q\). In this case we write \(P \equiv Q\).

Example 1.7: The Law of Contrapositive

Let \(P: p \Rightarrow q\) and \(Q: \neg q \Rightarrow \neg p\).

pq¬p¬qp ⇒ q¬q ⇒ ¬p
TTFFTT
TFFTFF
FTTFTT
FFTTTT

Columns 5 and 6 are identical. Therefore, \(p \Rightarrow q \equiv \neg q \Rightarrow \neg p\). This is the Law of Contrapositive, one of the most important equivalences in mathematics.

Example 1.8: A Non-Equivalence

Let \(P: p \Rightarrow q\) and \(Q: \neg p \Rightarrow \neg q\).

pq¬p¬qp ⇒ q¬p ⇒ ¬q
TTFFTT
TFFTFT
FTTFTF
FFTTTT

Columns 5 and 6 are not identical. Therefore, \(p \Rightarrow q \not\equiv \neg p \Rightarrow \neg q\).

📌 Cheat Notes: Key Points to Remember (Part 2)

  • Compound Proposition: Formed by joining two or more propositions with connectives.
  • Order of Operations: \(\neg\) first, then \(\wedge\) and \(\vee\), then \(\Rightarrow\) and \(\Leftrightarrow\).
  • Truth Table Rows: For \(n\) propositions, there are \(2^n\) rows.
  • Logical Equivalence: \(P\) and \(Q\) are equivalent (\(P \equiv Q\)) if their truth tables are identical.
  • Law of Contrapositive: \(p \Rightarrow q \equiv \neg q \Rightarrow \neg p\). This is the most important equivalence to memorize.
