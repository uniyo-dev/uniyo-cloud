3.8 Adjoint and Inverse of a Matrix

The inverses of matrices are useful to solve linear systems. In this section, we define the inverse of a matrix, discuss different methods to compute an inverse, and the properties of inverses.

📌 Definition 3.26 (Adjoint of a Matrix):

Let \(A\) be an \(n imes n\) matrix. If \([C_{ij}(A)]\) denotes the matrix of cofactors for \(A\), then the adjugate (or adjoint) matrix of \(A\), denoted by \( ext{Adj}(A)\), is defined by the formula:

\[ ext{Adj}(A) = [C_{ij}(A)]^t \]

That is, the adjoint of matrix \(A\) is the transpose of the matrix of cofactors for \(A\).

Example 3.41: Compute the adjoint of \(A = egin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\).

The matrix of cofactors is \(egin{bmatrix} 4 & -3 \ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Thus, \( ext{Adj}(A) = egin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

Inverse of a Matrix

📌 Definition 3.27:

The inverse of a square matrix \(A\) is an \(n imes n\) matrix \(B\) such that:

\[ AB = I_n = BA, \]

where \(I_n\) is the \(n imes n\) identity matrix. If such a matrix \(B\) exists, then \(A\) is said to be invertible (or nonsingular), and its inverse is denoted by \(A^{-1}\) (i.e., \(B = A^{-1}\)). A matrix that does not have an inverse is said to be noninvertible (or singular).

📌 Theorem 3.11:

Let \(A\) be an \(n imes n\) matrix. If \(A\) is invertible (nonsingular), then \(\det(A) eq 0\), and the inverse \(A^{-1}\) is given by the formula:

\[ A^{-1} = rac{1}{\det(A)} ext{Adj}(A). \]

Gauss-Jordan Elimination (Computing the Inverse)

Let \(A\) be an \(n imes n\) invertible matrix and \(I_n\) be the identity matrix of size \(n imes n\). The inverse \(A^{-1}\) can be obtained using elementary row operations as follows:

  1. Write the \(n imes 2n\) matrix that consists of \(A\) on the left and the \(n imes n\) identity matrix \(I_n\) on the right to obtain \([A \mid I_n]\).
  2. If possible, row reduce \(A\) to \(I_n\) using elementary row operations on the entire matrix \([A \mid I_n]\). The result will be the matrix \([I_n \mid A^{-1}]\). If this is not possible, then \(A\) is noninvertible (or singular).
  3. Check your work by multiplying to see that \(AA^{-1} = I_n = A^{-1}A\).

Example 3.45: Compute the inverse of \(A = egin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) using Gauss-Jordan elimination.

\[ [A \mid I_2] = egin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 1 & 0 \ 3 & 2 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Apply \(R_2 o R_2 - 3R_1\), then \(R_2 o rac{1}{5}R_2\), then \(R_1 o R_1 + R_2\):

\[ egin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2/5 & 1/5 \ 0 & 1 & -3/5 & 1/5 \end{bmatrix} \]

Thus, \(A^{-1} = egin{bmatrix} 2/5 & 1/5 \ -3/5 & 1/5 \end{bmatrix}\).

Laws of Inverse

📌 Theorem 3.12:

Let \(A, B, C\) be matrices of appropriate sizes, \(I\) the identity matrix, and \(lpha\) a nonzero scalar.

  1. If \(A\) is invertible, it has one and only one inverse, \(A^{-1}\).
  2. If \(A\) is invertible, then so is \(A^{-1}\), and \((A^{-1})^{-1} = A\).
  3. If any two of \(A, B, AB\) are invertible, then so is the third, and \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\).
  4. If \(A\) is invertible, then so is \(lpha A\), and \((lpha A)^{-1} = rac{1}{lpha} A^{-1}\).
  5. If \(A\) is invertible, then so is \(A^t\), and \((A^t)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^t\).
  6. If \(A\) is invertible, then \(AB = AC\) or \(BA = CA \Rightarrow B = C\).

📌 Cheat Notes: Key Points to Remember (Part 6)

  • Invertibility Condition: \(\det(A) eq 0\).

  • Gauss-Jordan: Row reduce \([A \mid I_n]\) to \([I_n \mid A^{-1}]\).
  • Key Properties: \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\), \((A^t)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^t\), \((lpha A)^{-1} = rac{1}{lpha} A^{-1}\).
