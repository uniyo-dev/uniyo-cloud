Part 1: The Real Number System, Properties, and Intervals

📖 MODULE OVERVIEW & LEARNING OBJECTIVES:

Our everyday lives are filled with situations in which we encounter relationships between two sets. To apply mathematics to a variety of disciplines, we must first make the idea of a "relationship" mathematically precise. Before we can define a function, we must rigorously define the Real Number System (\(\mathbb{R}\)).

In this part, we review the structure of the real numbers, their algebraic properties, and how they are organized on the number line and into intervals. This is the essential language for calculus and higher mathematics.

  • Identify the distinction between rational and irrational numbers.
  • Apply the 11 basic properties of real numbers (Commutative, Associative, Distributive, etc.).
  • Use the order relation and its properties to compare numbers.
  • Define and use interval notation effectively.

2.1 The Real Number Systems

The Integers and the Rational Numbers:

The simplest numbers are the natural numbers (\(\mathbb{N}\)), used for counting: \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots\). If we adjoin their negatives and zero, we obtain the integers (\(\mathbb{Z}\)): \(\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\).

When we try to measure length, weight, or voltage, the integers are inadequate—they are spaced too far apart. We are led to consider quotients (ratios) of integers, called rational numbers (\(\mathbb{Q}\)).

📌 Definition of a Rational Number:

Numbers which can be written in the form \(\frac{m}{n}\), where \(m\) and \(n\) are integers with \(n \neq 0\), are called rational numbers.

⚠️ CRITICAL EXAM WARNING:

Note that we included \(\frac{16}{2}\) and \(\frac{-17}{1}\) in the definition, though we normally write them as \(8\) and \(-17\).

Division by zero is undefined. We cannot divide by zero because there is no number that, when multiplied by 0, gives a non-zero result. Let us agree once and for all to banish division by zero from this section!

The Irrational Numbers:

Do the rational numbers serve to measure all lengths? No. This surprising fact was discovered by the ancient Greeks. They showed that while \(\sqrt{2}\) measures the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides of length 1, it cannot be written as a quotient of two integers. Thus, \(\sqrt{2}\) is an irrational (not rational) number. So are \(\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5}, \sqrt[3]{7}, \pi\), and a host of other numbers.

The Real Numbers (\(\mathbb{R}\)):

Consider the set of all numbers (rational and irrational) that can measure lengths, together with their negatives and zero. We call these numbers the real numbers. The set \(\mathbb{R}\) can be described as the union of the set of rational and irrational numbers.

The Four Arithmetic Operations

Given two real numbers \(x\) and \(y\), we may add or multiply them to obtain two new real numbers \(x + y\) and \(x \cdot y\) (also written \(xy\)). The real numbers, along with the operations of addition (+) and multiplication (\(\cdot\)), obey the 11 basic algebraic properties listed below.

PropertyOperationRuleExample
CommutativeAddition\(a + b = b + a\)\(5 + 2 = 2 + 5\)
Multiplication\(ab = ba\)\(5 \cdot 2 = 2 \cdot 5\)
AssociativeAddition\(a + (b + c) = (a + b) + c\)\(1 + (2 + 3) = (1 + 2) + 3\)
Multiplication\(a(bc) = (ab)c\)\(2 \cdot (3 \cdot 4) = (2 \cdot 3) \cdot 4\)
Distributive\(a(b + c) = ab + ac\)\(2(x + 3) = 2x + 6\)

Multiplicative (1)\(a \cdot 1 = a = 1 \cdot a\)\(7 \cdot 1 = 7\)
InverseAdditive (\(-a\))\(a + (-a) = 0 = (-a) + a\)\(8 + (-8) = 0\)
Multiplicative (\(\frac{1}{a}\))\(a \cdot (\frac{1}{a}) = 1\) (for \(a \neq 0\))\(4 \cdot (\frac{1}{4}) = 1\)
ClosureAdditionThe sum of two real numbers is a real number.\(2 + 3 = 5\)
MultiplicationThe product of two real numbers is a real number.\(2 \cdot 3 = 6\)

Subtraction and division are defined in terms of addition and multiplication:

\[x - y = x + (-y) \quad \text{and} \quad x \div y = x \cdot \frac{1}{y}, \quad \text{where } y \neq 0.\]

The Order Relation on the Set of Real Numbers

The nonzero real numbers separate nicely into two disjoint sets—the positive real numbers and the negative real numbers. This fact allows us to introduce the order relation \(<\) (read "is less than") by:

\[x < y \iff y - x \text{ is positive}.\]

The order relation \(<\) has the following properties (which are essential for solving inequalities):

PropertyRuleExample
TrichotomyFor any \(x, y\), exactly one holds: \(x < y, x = y,\) or \(x > y\).\(2 < 5\), \(2 = 2\), \(5 > 2\)
Transitivity\(x < y\) and \(y < z \implies x < z\).\(2 < 5\) and \(5 < 7 \implies 2 < 7\)
Addition\(x < y \iff x + z < y + z\).\(2 < 5 \implies 2 + 3 < 5 + 3\)
MultiplicationWhen \(z > 0\), \(x < y \iff xz < yz\).
When \(z < 0\), \(x < y \iff xz > yz\) (Reverse the symbol!).		\(2 < 5, z=3 \implies 6 < 15\).
\(2 < 5, z=-3 \implies -6 > -15\)

Intervals

In calculus and algebra, we often need to refer to specific subsets of the real number line. These are called intervals. Let \(a\) and \(b\) be two real numbers such that \(a < b\).

TypeNotationSet-Builder FormVisual Meaning
Open \((a, b)\) \(\{x \mid a < x < b\}\) Excludes both endpoints.
Closed \([a, b]\) \(\{x \mid a \leq x \leq b\}\) Includes both endpoints.
Open-Closed \((a, b]\) \(\{x \mid a < x \leq b\}\) Excludes \(a\), includes \(b\).
Closed-Open \([a, b)\) \(\{x \mid a \leq x < b\}\) Includes \(a\), excludes \(b\).

📌 Cheat Notes: Key Points to Remember

  • Number Sets: \(\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}\). Irrationals (\(\mathbb{I}\)) are the missing piece.
  • Division by Zero: \(\frac{5}{0}\) is undefined. \(\frac{0}{5} = 0\). \(\frac{0}{0}\) is indeterminate.
  • Closure Property Trap: The set of irrational numbers is not closed under addition or multiplication. Example: \(\sqrt{2} + (-\sqrt{2}) = 0\) (rational).
  • Interval Notation: A bracket \([\) or \(]\) means "included". A parenthesis \((\) or \()\) means "excluded".
  • Negative Multiplication: When multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative number, you MUST reverse the inequality symbol!
