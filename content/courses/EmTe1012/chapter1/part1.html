What is Emerging Technology?

The term "emerging technology" is generally used to describe a brand-new technology, but it may also refer to the continuing development of an already existing technology. The meaning of this term can shift slightly depending on the field in which it is used—whether in media, business, science, or education. In modern usage, it commonly refers to technologies that are currently under development, or those that are expected to become commercially available within the next five to ten years. Importantly, this term is usually reserved for technologies that are creating—or are expected to create—significant social or economic effects on human life.

Examples of emerging technologies include Artificial Intelligence (AI), Blockchain, Virtual Reality, 5G Networks, Autonomous Vehicles, and Internet of Things (IoT). These are not just gadgets; they are transforming entire industries, creating new jobs, and reshaping how we communicate, work, and live.

The Etymology of "Technology" and "Evolution":

  • Technology: This term has its origins in the 1610s, derived from the Greek word tekhnologia, which translates to "discourse or treatise on an art, craft, or technique." Originally, it referred to grammar. The root tekhnē (see techno-) combined with -logy (study of) gives it the meaning of "systematic treatment of an art or craft." The specific meaning we use today—"science of the mechanical and industrial arts"—was first recorded in 1859, during the height of the Industrial Revolution.
  • Evolution: This noun comes from the Latin word evolutio, meaning "an unrolling or opening". It is formed by combining the prefix e- (meaning "out") and the verb volvere (meaning "to roll"). Therefore, evolution literally means "rolling out." In the context of technology, it refers to the process of developing by gradual changes over time—improving step by step, generation after generation.

💡 Real World Example (Ethiopia):

In Ethiopia, the evolution of technology can be seen in the banking sector. We moved from manual ledger books to Core Banking Systems, then to ATM networks, and now to mobile money platforms like TeleBirr. TeleBirr is an emerging technology in Ethiopia—it is currently developing, has rapidly grown, and has created a significant economic impact by allowing millions of unbanked citizens to transfer money, pay bills, and save digitally using just a basic mobile phone.

1.1.1 Introduction to the Industrial Revolution (IR)

🔑 Activity 1.3 (From Module):

The Industrial Revolution (IR) was a profound period of major industrialization and innovation that took place during the late 1700s and early 1800s. But what truly defines an Industrial Revolution? At its core, an Industrial Revolution occurs when a society shifts from using basic tools to make products, to using new sources of energy—such as coal and later electricity—to power machines in factories. This shift fundamentally changed how goods were produced, how people worked, and where they lived.

The revolution began in Great Britain (England) in the late 1770s. It was driven by a series of technological innovations designed to make labor more efficient and productive. Before this era, manufacturing was done in small workshops and homes (the "cottage industry" system). With the invention of machines, production moved to large factories, which could produce massive quantities of goods in a fraction of the time.

Social and Cultural Transformation: This shift from home to factory brought about enormous changes in culture and society. People migrated from rural agricultural areas to big industrial cities in search of work in the factories. This rural-to-urban migration fundamentally transformed the social fabric, creating new social classes (the industrial working class and the factory-owning bourgeoisie) and new urban centers.

The American Industrial Revolution (Second IR): Commonly referred to as the Second Industrial Revolution, this phase started sometime between 1820 and 1870 in the United States. The impact was widespread. Industries such as textile manufacturing, mining, glass making, and agriculture all underwent massive transformations. For example, before the Industrial Revolution, textiles were primarily made of wool and were spun and woven by hand (a slow and labor-intensive process). With the invention of the Cotton Gin and mechanized looms, textile production skyrocketed, making clothing cheaper and more accessible to the masses.

1.1.2 The Most Important Inventions of the Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution introduced a plethora of inventions that fundamentally changed the world. These inventions can be categorized into three main areas: Transportation, Communication, and Industry.

CategoryInventionSignificance & Impact
Transportation The Steam Engine Developed by Thomas Newcomen and improved by James Watt. It provided the first reliable source of mechanical power that didn't depend on water or wind. It powered trains and ships, enabling rapid transport of goods and people.
The Railroad The invention of the steam locomotive allowed for the construction of transcontinental railways. This drastically reduced travel time, allowed for the mass transport of raw materials (like coal and iron) to factories, and accelerated the expansion of nations like the USA.
The Diesel Engine Invented by Rudolf Diesel. This engine was more efficient than steam engines and eventually became the standard for heavy-duty transportation (trucks, ships, locomotives).
The Airplane While the Wright brothers flew the first airplane in 1903 (late IR era), it represents the culmination of the age of mechanical transportation. It shrank the world and connected continents in hours rather than weeks.
Communication The Telegraph Invented by Samuel Morse. This was the first invention that allowed messages to be sent instantly over long distances using Morse Code. It revolutionized business and journalism.
The Transatlantic Cable Laid across the Atlantic Ocean, this physically connected the telegraph networks of North America and Europe. For the first time, a message sent from New York could be received in London within minutes instead of weeks by ship.
The Phonograph Invented by Thomas Edison. This was the first device capable of recording and playing back sound. It laid the foundation for the modern music and audio recording industry.
The Telephone Invented by Alexander Graham Bell. It allowed for real-time voice communication over long distances. This fundamentally changed how people conducted business, how families stayed in touch, and how emergency services were dispatched.
Industry The Cotton Gin Invented by Eli Whitney. This machine quickly and easily separated cotton fibers from their seeds, making cotton production enormously profitable in the American South and fueling the textile industry in England.
The Sewing Machine Invented by Elias Howe and Isaac Singer. This revolutionized the textile and clothing industry, making mass production of ready-to-wear clothing possible and significantly reducing the cost of garments for ordinary people.
Electric Lights Invented by Thomas Edison (and independently by Joseph Swan). The incandescent light bulb extended the productive day into the night, transforming factories, homes, and cities. It was the first practical application of electricity to daily life.

1.1.3 Historical Background (IR 1.0, IR 2.0, IR 3.0)

The Beginning in Great Britain: The Industrial Revolution began in Great Britain in the late 1770s. Why Britain? Because it had abundant coal and iron ore (the raw materials for machines), a strong navy to protect trade routes, a stable political system, and a vast colonial empire that provided both raw materials and markets for finished goods. Furthermore, the Agricultural Revolution had led to an increase in food production. This meant that fewer farmers were needed to feed the population, and many displaced workers moved to the cities to work in the new factories.

Spread to Europe: The revolution quickly spread to mainland Europe. The first European countries to be industrialized after England were Belgium, France, and the German states. These regions had resources similar to Britain's and quickly adopted the new manufacturing techniques.

The Agricultural Revolution's Role: As stated, the final cause of the Industrial Revolution was the effects created by the Agricultural Revolution. The introduction of new farming techniques (like crop rotation and the seed drill) and new livestock breeding methods led to a massive increase in food production. With a stable and abundant food supply, the population grew rapidly, and surplus labor was available to work in the new industrial factories. Thus, the Industrial Revolution was born out of the success of the Agricultural Revolution.

The Four Types of Industries:

The module categorizes the economy into four distinct industry types. Understanding these is crucial to grasping the economic shifts of the Industrial Revolution.

Industry TypeFunctionExamples (Ethiopian Context)
Primary Industry Involves getting raw materials directly from the earth or nature. Ethiopia: Coffee farming, gold mining, fishing in Lake Tana, and cotton farming.
Secondary Industry Involves manufacturing—taking raw materials and turning them into finished products. Ethiopia: Textile factories in Hawassa Industrial Park, steel production, cement factories, and food processing plants.
Tertiary Industry Provides services rather than products. Ethiopia: Teachers in Addis Ababa, nurses in hospitals, bankers in commercial banks, and retail shop owners.
Quaternary Industry Involves research and development (R&D) and knowledge-based activities. Ethiopia: IT developers creating software for e-commerce, AI research at Addis Ababa University, and tech startups in the iCog Labs incubator.

Industrial Revolution 3.0 (IR 3.0) - The Digital Revolution

Then came the Third Industrial Revolution (IR 3.0). Unlike IR 1.0 (mechanical) and IR 2.0 (electrical), IR 3.0 introduced the transition from mechanical and analog electronic technology to digital electronics. This era began in the late 1950s with the invention of the transistor and the integrated circuit.

Due to the massive shift towards digitalization, IR 3.0 was given the nickname: "The Digital Revolution". The core factor of this revolution was the mass production and widespread use of digital logic circuits. This led to the creation of derived technologies such as:

  • The Computer: From room-sized mainframes to personal computers (PCs).
  • Handphones (Mobile Phones): Making communication portable and accessible.
  • The Internet: Connecting billions of devices and people globally.

The Transformative Power of IR 3.0: These technological innovations have arguably transformed traditional production and business techniques. Before the internet, businesses relied on physical mail and fax machines. Today, emails, video conferencing, and cloud computing allow people to communicate and collaborate without the need of being physically present.

Legacy of IR 3.0: Certain practices that were enabled during IR 3.0 are still being heavily practiced today. For example, the proliferation of digital computers and the shift to digital records (replacing paper files) is now the standard in businesses, governments, and hospitals worldwide.

📌 The Bridge to IR 4.0:

The module summarizes the journey beautifully: "From the first industrial revolution (mechanization through water and steam power) to the mass production and assembly lines using electricity in the second, the fourth industrial revolution will take what was started in the third with the adoption of computers and automation and enhance it with smart and autonomous systems fueled by data and machine learning."

📌 Cheat Notes: Key Points to Remember (Part 1)

  • Emerging Tech: New or developing technologies with significant social/economic impact.
  • Technology (Etymology): From Greek tekhnologia (systematic treatment of an art/craft).
  • Evolution (Etymology): From Latin evolutio (an unrolling).
  • IR 1.0: Started in Great Britain (1770s). Mechanization using water and steam power. Shift from homes to factories.
  • IR 2.0: American IR (1820-1870). Mass production using electricity and assembly lines.
  • IR 3.0: Digital Revolution (late 1950s). Digital logic, computers, internet, mobile phones.
  • 4 Industry Types: Primary (raw materials), Secondary (manufacturing), Tertiary (services), Quaternary (R&D/IT).
  • IR 4.0: Cyber-physical systems, AI, IoT, robotics (coined by Klaus Schwab in 2016).
